Good morning. We will cover autoimmune hepatitis: how to recognise it, establish the diagnosis and understand treatment. The theme is a treatable cause of otherwise unexplained hepatitis.

A treatable loss of tolerance

  • InflammationImmune-mediated injury to hepatocytes
  • CluesAutoantibodies, raised IgG and compatible histology
  • ConsequenceUntreated disease can progress to cirrhosis

AASLD practice guidance 2019 (published 2020).

AIH is an inflammatory liver disease of uncertain cause. It is often chronic and fluctuating, but its first presentation may be acute. There is no single diagnostic test.

If asked: Is it an antibody-mediated disease? T-cell responses are central to hepatocyte injury. Autoantibodies are mainly diagnostic markers; their presence alone does not establish AIH.

If asked: Why “lupoid hepatitis”? This is a historical name associated with ANA positivity; AIH is not simply hepatic lupus.

The overall female predominance is approximately 3:1. Traditional teaching describes peaks in young people and middle age, but the age distribution varies: older age must not discourage investigation.

Ask about autoimmune disease in the patient and family. The next slide compares the traditional serological types, including their sex distribution.

If asked: Does AIH occur in men? Yes. Demographics change suspicion; they cannot exclude the diagnosis. Avoid treating the overall sex ratio as a fixed ratio for each subtype.

This is the traditional textbook comparison. ANA means antinuclear antibody; SMA, smooth-muscle antibody; LKM, liver–kidney microsomal antibody; LC, liver cytosol antibody. Both forms affect girls/women more often.

If asked: Why these ratios? The AASLD teaching table gives approximately 4:1 and 10:1. These are reference-specific teaching estimates, not universal constants: paediatric cohorts and other guidance report different proportions.

Type 2 often presents younger and may present acutely. Do not promise a uniformly worse prognosis solely from antibody type. IgA deficiency is an association worth knowing.

If asked: What about type 3? Anti-SLA/LP positivity was historically called type 3, but it is not regarded as a separate established type.

Current update: EASL 2025 does not recommend autoantibody-based subclassification in adults for clinical management. Keep the traditional types for learning, while explaining that severity and response guide treatment.

How tolerance breaks

Genetic soil

HLA-associated susceptibility makes loss of tolerance more likely.

HLA-DRB1*03:01 / *04:01 are classic associations; risk alleles vary by ancestry.

A possible trigger

An environmental exposure may initiate autoimmunity in a susceptible host.

Molecular mimicry and gut–liver signalling are proposed mechanisms; no single trigger explains AIH.

Self-antigen is presented

Antigen-presenting cells display liver self-antigens to CD4⁺ T cells.

The brakes fail

Regulatory control becomes insufficient to restrain autoreactive immune responses.

Hepatocytes are attacked

Helper-cell cytokines sustain inflammation. Cytotoxic T cells injure hepatocytes.

B cells join in

B cells become plasma cells, producing IgG and the autoantibodies we measure.

A self-sustaining loop

Injury releases more antigen, sustaining inflammation and eventually fibrosis.

Mieli-Vergani et al., Nat Rev Dis Primers 2018; Sîrbe et al., Int J Mol Sci 2021; de Boer et al., Gastroenterology 2014; Kerkar et al., J Immunol 2003; Longhi et al., J Hepatol 2004.

Keep this as a quick visual sequence: susceptibility → possible trigger → antigen presentation → failed regulation → T-cell injury → plasma-cell response → continuing injury. Seven reveals, about 60–90 seconds overall.

Genetics: HLA associations support susceptibility, not Mendelian inheritance. DR3/DR4 associations are not universal across ethnic groups. AIRE mutations in APECED are a special setting, not the explanation for ordinary AIH.

Triggers: Molecular mimicry is a hypothesis supported by experimental observations. Do not say that HCV or HSV is a proven cause of idiopathic AIH. Drug-induced autoimmune-like hepatitis is considered separately.

Regulation: T-regulatory-cell findings vary with disease state and study method; a uniform numerical deficiency is not established in every patient.

Clinical link: Hepatocyte injury raises aminotransferases; plasma-cell activity helps explain raised IgG. Autoantibodies are primarily markers. Interface inflammation and fibrosis are assessed on biopsy.

AASLD practice guidance 2019; BSG guideline 2025.

AIH spans asymptomatic abnormal tests, chronic nonspecific illness, acute hepatitis and liver failure. Nausea, anorexia, abdominal discomfort and jaundice may accompany an acute presentation.

Acute presentation can be new disease or a flare of previously unrecognised chronic disease. Assess severity immediately rather than waiting for antibody results.

If asked: Can normal IgG exclude AIH? No. Normal IgG and negative conventional antibodies can occur, particularly in acute disease.

An examination may be normal. Look for jaundice, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly and stigmata of chronic liver disease; none is specific to AIH.

If asked: Does a very high ALT mean liver failure? ALT/AST reflect injury, not synthetic capacity. INR is especially important in acute illness. Albumin is useful in chronic assessment but also changes with nutrition and other illness.

Confusion, asterixis, bleeding or ascites change the urgency. The acute severe pathway is covered near the end.

Mack et al., AASLD guidance, Hepatology 2020; EASL CPG 2015; Mieli-Vergani et al., Nat Rev Dis Primers 2018.

Begin with the pattern of injury. ALT and AST usually rise more than ALP. Serum IgG is often selectively raised; measure IgG rather than relying only on total globulin.

ANA and SMA are useful but occur in other diseases and sometimes in healthy people. Anti-SLA/LP is highly specific but is absent in most patients. Seronegative AIH is possible after careful exclusion of alternatives.

If asked: What titre matters? In the original adult simplified score, ANA/SMA ≥1:40 gives one point and ≥1:80 gives two. Assay method matters; children have different thresholds.

If asked: What if ALP is markedly raised? Investigate obstruction and cholestatic disease. Consider a PBC/PSC variant, rather than assuming typical AIH.

Use this sequence to make the next slides intelligible: suspicion, investigation, biopsy and synthesis. Ultrasound evaluates liver morphology and obstruction. Take a full drug, supplement and alcohol history.

In an acute jaundiced patient, include hepatitis A and E testing as appropriate in addition to HBV and HCV. Evaluate Wilson disease in younger patients; tailor other tests to the presentation.

If asked: Is the score mandatory? It supports an expert clinical diagnosis. It is less reliable in acute, seronegative and variant disease; a low score does not safely exclude these.

If asked: What if biopsy is unsafe? Discuss an alternative route, such as transjugular biopsy, with the specialist team. Do not delay urgent management of liver failure.

Use the 3D lobule to locate the portal tract and limiting plate. Interface hepatitis means inflammation crossing that boundary to injure adjacent hepatocytes; the older term is piecemeal necrosis.

Plasma-cell-rich inflammation supports AIH but plasma cells may be sparse. Acute AIH can have prominent lobular or centrilobular injury. Biopsy also grades inflammation, stages fibrosis and evaluates competing explanations.

If asked: Are rosettes and emperipolesis pathognomonic? No. Both can accompany severe injury from other causes. The 2022 pathology consensus places more weight on the pattern of inflammation and competing features.

If asked: Why do they appear in the score? The original 2008 score used an older histological definition; do not confuse that definition with current pathology consensus.

Original simplified adult IAIHG score, 2008. ≥6 probable; ≥7 definite by criteria. Antibody points capped at 2.

Invite the class to score this teaching example. ANA 1:160 gives 2; IgG 1.5 × ULN gives 2; typical histology gives 2; exclusion of viral hepatitis gives 2. Total 8: definite AIH by the simplified criteria. Spend about 45 seconds here.

Antibody points are not additive: the maximum is 2 even if several antibodies are positive. For IgG, >ULN gives 1 and >1.1 × ULN gives 2.

Original 2008 histology definition: Typical requires interface hepatitis with lymphocytic/lymphoplasmacytic portal inflammation extending into the lobule, rosettes AND emperipolesis. Compatible means a chronic hepatitis pattern without all typical features. Modern histological assessment is broader.

If asked: Does a score below 6 rule it out? No. This is the original adult teaching score, not a paediatric or overlap calculator. Acute and seronegative cases may be missed. Assay-specific updated criteria also exist.

BSG guideline 2025. Additional testing depends on age and presentation.

The purpose is to test competing explanations, not simply collect negative results. Viral, metabolic and autoimmune liver disease may coexist.

Viral: Use appropriate serology and nucleic-acid tests according to timing and exposure. Consider HEV in acute hepatitis, particularly in our setting.

Drugs: Drug-induced autoimmune-like hepatitis can resemble AIH in blood tests and biopsy. Improvement after drug withdrawal and lack of relapse after stopping immunosuppression favour it, but neither pattern is absolute. Checkpoint-inhibitor hepatitis is a related immune-mediated injury, not automatically classical AIH.

Wilson: Caeruloplasmin alone cannot exclude it. Use the combined clinical and laboratory assessment.

Variants: AMA suggests PBC; cholangiography assesses PSC when indicated. A marked cholestatic pattern warrants a different diagnostic pathway. Backup B covers the main distinctions.

Adult treatment: EASL 2025; response definitions: IAIHG 2022. Detailed regimens in backup A.

Treat active disease, including many patients who have few or no symptoms. Symptoms alone do not determine the need for treatment. Mild disease needs an individual specialist decision.

Prednisolone acts rapidly; azathioprine acts more slowly and reduces long-term steroid exposure. Do not use azathioprine alone to induce remission. Continue maintenance immunosuppression after steroid tapering.

Current update: EASL 2025 also supports prednisolone plus mycophenolate as an adult first-line option. MMF is teratogenic. Budesonide is no longer recommended as first-line by EASL 2025 and is contraindicated in cirrhosis.

Before azathioprine: Baseline blood count, liver tests and TPMT assessment; pharmacogenetic assessment according to local practice. Screen for relevant infection risks, review vaccination and assess bone health. Detailed doses and adverse effects are in backup A.

If asked: Is improvement enough? No. The target is normal aminotransferases and IgG, rather than merely a fall from baseline.

BSG 2025; AASLD 2019. Withdrawal requires specialist assessment and continued monitoring.

Clinical improvement can precede biochemical remission, and biochemical remission can precede histological remission. Do not stop therapy when symptoms settle.

Monitor liver tests and blood counts frequently during initiation and dose changes; intervals can widen after stability. Review blood pressure, glucose, infection, bone health and medication tolerance.

If asked: What if response is inadequate? Review the diagnosis, adherence, dose and competing causes of liver injury before changing drugs. MMF and tacrolimus are specialist alternatives depending on intolerance or insufficient response.

If asked: When can all treatment stop? Consider only selected patients with sustained normal aminotransferases and IgG for at least two years. It is not an automatic stop date. Histological assessment may help, especially when residual activity is a concern. Relapse often means prolonged or lifelong treatment.

If asked: Prognosis? Sustained control reduces disease progression, but cirrhosis and persistent inflammation worsen outlook. Avoid using historical trial mortality as a modern individual prognosis.

BSG 2025; AASLD 2019; EASL 2025. Acute severity definitions vary slightly between guidelines.

Acute severe disease: In a patient without established chronic liver disease, acute jaundice with coagulopathy and no encephalopathy suggests acute severe AIH. Encephalopathy indicates AIH-related acute liver failure and requires immediate transplant-centre evaluation.

A supervised steroid trial may be appropriate in acute severe AIH after infection assessment. Reassess promptly; do not wait for a fixed seven-day deadline if the patient deteriorates. Lack of improvement over 3–7 days is a major escalation signal, not automatic transplant listing.

Pregnancy: Plan conception during controlled disease. Do not abruptly stop prednisolone or azathioprine; ongoing treatment is often appropriate. MMF must be avoided with specialist preconception planning. Postpartum flares need monitoring.

Cirrhosis: Continue appropriate disease control, assess for portal hypertension and arrange six-monthly liver ultrasound with or without AFP according to the surveillance protocol. Decompensation may require transplant assessment. Budesonide is contraindicated.

Five things to remember

  • Consider AIH in unexplained hepatitis at any age
  • Combine IgG, antibodies, exclusions and biopsy
  • Normal IgG or negative antibodies do not rule it out
  • Treat to normal AST, ALT and IgG; maintain control
  • Recognise liver failure early; expect long-term follow-up

Use these five sentences as the recap. AIH is treatable, but recognition, a combined diagnosis and sustained control are essential.

EASL 2025 adult regimens; individualise with specialist oversight. Paediatric doses differ.

These are adult teaching ranges, not a one-size-fits-all prescription. EASL 2025 starts prednisolone at least 0.5 mg/kg/day and up to 1 mg/kg/day in more severe disease.

Azathioprine is ideally introduced about two weeks after steroids, when bilirubin is below 6 mg/dL. A typical starting dose is 50 mg/day, increasing towards 1–2 mg/kg/day as tolerated. It is not used alone for induction.

Check baseline blood count, liver tests and TPMT before azathioprine. Normal TPMT does not remove the need for blood-count monitoring. Pharmacogenetic testing follows local availability and practice.

MMF is an adult first-line alternative in EASL 2025 and is also used for azathioprine intolerance. Counsel about teratogenicity, contraception and planned conception. Tacrolimus is reserved for selected specialist-managed cases.

Budesonide was an option in older guidance, but EASL 2025 no longer recommends it as first-line. It is contraindicated in cirrhosis and unsuitable in acute severe disease.

BSG guideline 2025, variant / overlap syndromes; EASL 2025.

Variants combine hepatitic and cholestatic features. PBC is primary biliary cholangitis; PSC is primary sclerosing cholangitis; UDCA is ursodeoxycholic acid.

Consider AIH–PBC when a patient has AIH features plus cholestatic biochemistry, AMA or characteristic bile-duct lesions. Biopsy and assessment of the predominant component guide treatment.

If asked about Paris criteria: Classically at least two PBC features (ALP ≥2 × ULN or GGT ≥5 × ULN; AMA; florid duct lesion) and at least two AIH features (ALT ≥5 × ULN; IgG ≥2 × ULN or SMA; moderate/severe interface hepatitis), with interface hepatitis required. These criteria do not identify every variant.

For AIH–PSC, investigate bile ducts with MRCP when cholestasis, pruritus, IBD, biliary biopsy changes or poor response raise suspicion. Sclerosing cholangitis merits particular consideration in children; their workup differs from adult AIH.

Treat the dominant injury and consider both components. Do not assume that immunosuppression will treat established PSC duct disease.

These are historical treatment trials in severe disease, useful to explain why immunosuppression became standard. They are not modern all-patient survival estimates.

The Mayo trial showed lower mortality with prednisone alone or combined with azathioprine than with placebo or azathioprine alone. Azathioprine acts too slowly for induction.

The Royal Free trial demonstrated a long-term survival benefit from prednisolone. Modern prognosis depends on disease control, fibrosis, cirrhosis and other patient factors. Relapse estimates vary with the population and withdrawal criteria.
